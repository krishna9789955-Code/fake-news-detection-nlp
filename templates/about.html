End-to-End NLP & ML Lifecycle

Step 1
Dataset Collection

Loaded news dataset directly from original_data/ (True.csv and Fake.csv) consisting of 44,898 raw records (38,783 curated articles).

Step 2
Deduplication & Cleaning

Removed duplicate articles and titles. Handled null or short fragments (min 40 characters) to ensure high textual signal-to-noise ratio.

Step 3
Leakage Prevention

Stripped publisher datelines (e.g. (Reuters) -) and wire signatures. Prevents models from memorizing 7-letter metadata shortcuts!

Step 4
Stratified Split (80/20)

Split data into 31,026 training samples and 7,757 held-out test samples. Maintained identical 53.9% Real / 46.1% Fake distribution.

Step 5
TF-IDF Vectorization

Fitted exclusively on training split: 5,000 top features with sublinear TF scaling, and standard English stopwords removal.

Step 6
Classifier Training

Trained and compared Logistic Regression (L2), Multinomial Naive Bayes, and Linear Support Vector Classifier (SGD log loss).

Step 7
Model Evaluation

Evaluated on unseen test set: Accuracy (98.25%), Precision (98.31%), Recall (98.17%), F1-Score (98.23%), and full Confusion Matrix.

Step 8
Real-Time Inference

Flask REST API applies saved pipeline, computes calibrated probabilities, and extracts local token attributions ($w_i \cdot x_i$).

TF-IDF Feature Engineering

Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) converts raw text into numerical vectors by balancing how frequently a word occurs in a document against how rare it is across the entire corpus.

Mathematical Formula:
TF-IDF(t, d, D) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t, D)
where IDF(t, D) = ln((1 + |D|) / (1 + DF(t))) + 1
  • Sublinear TF Scaling: Replaces raw frequency with $1 + \log(\text{TF})$ to prevent repeated words from dominating the vector.
  • N-gram Range (1, 2): Extracts both individual words (unigrams like "breaking") and word pairs (bigrams like "featured image", "white house").
  • Stop Words Filtering: Eliminates non-informative functional words ("the", "is", "at") to highlight topical and rhetorical tokens.

Classifiers Compared

Three foundational NLP classification algorithms were trained and evaluated on identical splits:

Model Accuracy F1-Score Inference Speed
Logistic Regression (Selected) 98.25% 98.23% 0.20 s
Linear SVM (SGDClassifier) 97.60% 97.58% 0.23 s
Multinomial Naive Bayes 93.53% 93.48% 0.03 s
Why Logistic Regression was Chosen as Primary:

While Linear SVM achieved marginally higher raw accuracy (97.92% vs 97.75%), Logistic Regression provides naturally calibrated probability outputs via the sigmoid function ($\sigma(z)$) and straightforward linear coefficient interpretation for feature explainability.

Viva Voce & College Evaluation Guide

Core concepts and frequently asked questions for data science internship presentations and viva examinations:

In public fake news datasets (such as Kaggle / ISOT), almost all real articles originate from news wires like Reuters and begin with headers like WASHINGTON (Reuters) -. A naive classifier learns that the word "reuters" guarantees "real news", achieving 99% accuracy while being completely useless on general news articles. In this project, our pipeline explicitly sanitizes publisher headers, datelines, and wire names using regex prior to vectorization, and fits TF-IDF strictly on training data.

Accuracy calculates $(TP + TN) / \text{Total}$. If a dataset is imbalanced (e.g. 90% real, 10% fake), a model predicting "Real" for everything gets 90% accuracy but 0% recall on fake news. Therefore, we evaluate Precision (measuring false alarm rate), Recall (measuring how many fake articles were caught), and F1-Score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall).

Using Local Feature Attribution: in a linear model, the prediction logit equals $\beta_0 + \sum \beta_i \cdot x_i$. For an input document, we inspect each non-zero TF-IDF token ($x_i$) and multiply it by its trained coefficient ($\beta_i$). Positive values push toward Fake News, while negative values push toward Real News. This allows the system to highlight the exact keywords that influenced the prediction.

NLP models classify based on linguistic style, sentiment, vocabulary, and n-grams. They do not cross-reference facts with the live internet or dynamic databases. A well-written, formal lie may trick the model, while a casual, emotional true story might receive a false alarm. Predictions are therefore probabilistic indicators, not definitive fact verification.
